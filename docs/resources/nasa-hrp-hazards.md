# 5 Hazards of Human Spaceflight (NASA HRP)

- **Source:** https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/hazards/
- **Page last updated:** 2026-06-12
- **Extracted:** 2026-11-14. Short quotes are verbatim; the rest is paraphrased.

**Purpose:** NASA's Human Research Program (HRP) page that names the five hazards astronauts face in spaceflight, with one paragraph per hazard. Recognizing them lets NASA work on sending humans to the space station, the Moon, Mars and beyond.

## Overview

- The five hazards are space radiation, isolation and confinement, distance from Earth, gravity (including its absence), and hostile or closed environments.
- The hazards can "feed off one another" and worsen effects on the body.
- Ground-based analogs, laboratories and the International Space Station are used as test beds.

## 1. Space Radiation

- Space radiation is "invisible to the human eye" and is "considered one of the most hazardous aspects of spaceflight."
- It is the hazard "most difficult to visualize" for a human mission to Mars.

## 2. Isolation and Confinement

- Behavioral responses occur in groups isolated and confined in a small space for a long time.
- Crews must be carefully chosen, trained and supported to work as a team for "months or years in space."

## 3. Distance from Earth

- The deeper in space, the harder it is to receive instructions, supplies and medical care from Earth.
- Mars is on average 140 million miles from Earth.
- A trip to Mars would last roughly three years, compared with a three-day lunar trip.
- "Planning and self-sufficiency will be key to successful deep space missions."

## 4. Gravity Fields

- Muscles, bones, inner ear and organs must adjust to new gravity levels: in the spacecraft, on the Moon and Mars, and again on Earth after return.
- On Mars, astronauts would live and work in three-eighths of Earth's gravity for up to two years.

## 5. Hostile/Closed Environments

- A spacecraft is both a home and a machine; its ecosystem shapes daily life.
- A safe ecosystem means managing temperature, pressure and lighting; reducing noise; monitoring microbial communities; and tracking immune responses.

## Not on the page

- No radiation doses, exposure limits or durations.
- No medical effects of radiation.
- No numeric limits for temperature, pressure, noise or microbes.
- No specific countermeasures (exercise, shielding, medication). Only research, planning and crew selection are mentioned.
